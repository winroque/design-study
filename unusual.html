Unusual interfaces

Forty-two interfaces that break the mold: some became references, others failed instructively, and some are strange on purpose. Every specimen responds to touch, and every card points out what is worth borrowing for your own work.

Tap, hover and type: every specimen is interactive.

When the physical world invades the screen

2022 →· Nothing

Nothing OS

A dot-matrix font, monochrome with a single red, and the Glyph Interface: LEDs on the back of the phone that notify you while the screen faces down.

Borrow this
One accent color for everything that needs attention.

2011 →· OP-1 and friends

Teenage Engineering

Each color matches a physical knob; the screen is a tiny LCD. The toy-like look hides a rigorous mapping between color and function.

Borrow this
Color tied to function, always the same across the whole product.
1-bit
2022· Panic

Playdate

A console with a 1-bit screen, black and white only, and a crank as a controller. Games fake tones and depth with dither patterns.

Borrow this
Constraint as a visual language.
2017 →· Bret Victor

Dynamicland

The computer is the whole building. Sheets of paper with colored dots in the corners are read by cameras, and projectors draw light on them. Programming means moving paper around the table.

Borrow this
Computing that several people handle together, in the same space.
2008 →· Dead Space and others

Diegetic interface

The information exists inside the depicted world. The health bar is on the character’s spine, not in a corner of the screen. The idea carries over to augmented reality and car dashboards.

Borrow this
Information drawn on the object itself, like measurements on a photo or routes on a map.
2015· watchOS

Honeycomb

The Apple Watch app grid: the focus grows and the edges shrink, a fisheye applied to icons. Hover or tap.

Borrow this
Focus plus context on a small screen.

Group 3

New ways to give a command

1990s· Plan 9, Rob Pike

Acme

There is no menu. Any word on screen is executable, including the ones you typed yourself. Click “Look”, “Put” or “Del” in the blue bar.

Borrow this
Text as interface: commands that live inside the document.
menu
1988 →· Hopkins, Maya

Pie menu

Every option sits at the same distance from the cursor, as Fitts’s law predicts. In Autodesk Maya, experienced users do not even look: they learn the gesture and the menu becomes muscle memory.

Borrow this
Image editing or drawing tools that never take your eyes off the content.
2011 →· Sublime Text, VS Code, Linear

Command palette ⌘K

You type your intent instead of hunting for the command in a menu. Try typing “theme” or “ope” and use the arrows and Enter.

Borrow this
An app’s most used commands, all within reach of the keyboard.
1974 →· BBC Ceefax

Teletext

Navigation by page number, with four colored buttons on the remote. The page counter ticking up while the signal arrived was part of the experience.

Borrow this
Short, memorable addresses for screens people use often.

Infinite canvas

Navigation happens by zooming, not by pages. Zoom out to see the structure; zoom in to reveal the detail. Jef Raskin argued for it in the 1980s; tldraw, Muse and Miro made it common.

Borrow this
Overview and detail in the same space, without switching screens.

1996· Palm Pilot

Palm Graffiti

Instead of teaching the device to read your handwriting, Palm taught you to write each letter in a single stroke. It worked so well it became a lawsuit: Xerox won in court and Palm switched to Graffiti 2 in 2003.

Hamburger menu

Three lines that mean “there is more here”. Drawn by Norm Cox for the Xerox Star, it was forgotten for three decades and exploded with phones. Tap the three lines.

webOS cards

Each open app is a card; flicking the card up closes the app. Palm did not survive, but the gesture lives on in the iPhone and Android. Drag a card up, or tap to open it.

Borrow this
Representing open tasks as objects the hand can pick up and discard.

Categories run horizontally, items vertically, crossing at a fixed point. Built for a game controller, with no cursor and no touch, it was the face of the PlayStation 3. Tap the categories and the items.

Two-dimensional navigation for TVs and directional controllers.

2010 →· term coined by Harry Brignull

Dark patterns

Interfaces designed to deceive: a refusal that humiliates (“confirmshaming”), a pre-checked box, a huge yes and a hidden no. Today they are called deceptive patterns and are targeted by consumer-protection laws. Tap each part.

Lesson
Every choice should carry the same visual weight; no consent box comes pre-checked.

A collection of everything not to do, and a great exercise in critique.

1990s – 2000s· Windows and the web

Cursor trail

Windows had a serious pointer-trail option, meant for slow LCD screens where the cursor disappeared. The web turned it into little stars and sparkles behind the mouse.

Borrow this
A good reminder that every ornament was once the solution to a real problem.
2013 →· product pages

Scrolljacking

The page takes over the mouse wheel: you scroll down and the content moves sideways, in steps, at the pace the designer chose. Scroll over the specimen or tap it.

Lesson
Impressive the first time and irritating every time after. Never take scrolling away from the user.
2002 →· Minority Report and other films

Movie interfaces

Holographic panels flung by hand, spinning charts, everything glowing. Science-fiction interfaces, made to look futuristic on the big screen, inspired real products and plenty of frustration. Tap to shuffle.

Lesson
What impresses in a 10-second scene rarely works over an 8-hour workday.
